    Harley-Davidson: chasing a new generation of customers

    How to win young riders without losing the Core. A recommendation to the CMO, as seen in 2007–08.

    October 2026 | Individual report | Manuel Carreira
    Executive summary

    Harley should win young riders under its own name, with dark bikes it already makes, and sell Buell

    Harley-Davidson enters 2008 as the leader of the US heavyweight market1 (44.6% share), but its customers are ageing (median age 35 → 47 between 1987 and 2007) and young adults are not replacing them. They rate Harley a “Leadership” brand2, yet only 16% feel it is for them.

    Why this works

    Every product exists or arrives by month 18, so it fits the CMO’s window; it needs under $1 million of new capital, so it holds up in the crisis; and it serves the mission to fulfil dreams “through the experience of motorcycling” for “the general public”, while the Core keeps its chrome bikes and rallies.

    Part I

    Defining the problem

    1 · The problem

    Harley’s customers are ageing and the company is not replacing them, which threatens its future sales and its mission

    Harley-Davidson’s customers are ageing (median age 35 → 47 between 1987 and 2007), and the company is not recruiting enough young riders to replace them.Problem statement

    Sales are still strong, with a record year in 2006, but the ageing threatens future sales. The first warning sign is that Harley fell faster than the market in 2007 (−6.1% against −4.1%).

    Link to the mission

    “We fulfill dreams through the experience of motorcycling, by providing to motorcyclists and to the general public an expanding line of motorcycles and branded products and services in selected market segments.”11 A company whose purpose is to fulfil dreams for “the general public” with “an expanding line” cannot do so if its riders come from one generation that will age out of riding.

    2 · Industry environment

    The heavyweight industry is attractive for a brand leader, but it is cooling and Harley has started to lose ground faster than the market

    The market almost tripled in 1997–2006, but Harley grew more slowly, and in 2007 it fell faster than the market. The gap is specific to Harley, not just the economy.

    Conclusion. The industry is attractive for a brand-led leader, because entry is hard and loyalty high, but it is turning hostile: demand is falling, and for young riders the Japanese compete on price and speed. Harley should compete on its brand and community, not on the rivals’ terms.

    3 · Root causes: why young adults do not buy a Harley

    Young adults know and respect Harley; seven fixable causes stop them from becoming owners

    Young Adults (18–34) are the target: the case calls them “the biggest question hanging over the brand”, and Harley holds only 22% of them.

    The gap is relevance, not awareness: young adults rate Harley a “Leadership” brand, but do not feel it is for them. Seven causes explain why.

    Options rejected. A new youth motorcycle14 would arrive four years later, 21 months after the average CMO has left; Buell as the gateway does not make the Harley brand younger; emerging markets add growth but do not renew the US customer base. Prioritisation and timeline: Appendix A.

    Part II

    Proposed solution

    4 · The solution

    Win young riders under the Harley name, with dark bikes Harley already makes and a dealership that welcomes them

    Branded house (one name) or house of brands (Buell as the youth brand)? Harley’s brand equity and the portfolio matrix both favour the Harley name.

    For young adults who want to escape a constrained routine (target), Harley’s dark line is the authentic American motorcycle (what) that comes with a community to ride with, unlike mass-produced sport bikes (USP), so they can become who they want to be, from $6,699 (benefit), backed by a century of riding culture and 600,000 H.O.G. members (evidence).Positioning statement

    5 · What to do with Buell

    The $30 million 1125R destroyed value and keeping Buell loses money: in a crisis, Harley should sell it

    Buell carries no Harley name and sells fewer than 5,600 bikes a year in the US. A discounted cash flow (DCF) was built from the case data: Buell’s 5% gross margin, the 10% it hopes for, and the $30 million 1125R17. Cash flows are discounted at a WACC of 8.4% over 10 years (Appendix H).

    −$10.5mvalue of the 1125R in Harley’s own base case: building it was a mistake
    −$14.6mvalue of keeping Buell from now on: still a mistake
    35¢ vs 5¢gross profit per dollar of sales, Harley vs Buell

    The 1125R was a poor investment. Its value is negative even in the bull case (−$1.2m), and in Harley’s own base case it destroys $10.5m. Breaking even would need about 10,900 sales in year 1, almost twice Buell’s US volume. The bear and base cases stay negative at any discount rate from 6% to 12%.

    Keeping Buell is negative too, and the estimate is generous. To compete, Buell must keep launching models; we assume one 1125R-sized model every five years ($6m a year), while Suzuki and Yamaha update their flagship sport bikes every two years and replace them every four to five18. So the real cost is higher than the −$14.6m shown.

    The crisis makes it worse. By September 2007 Harley’s stock was down 20%, and in the downturn it “saw sales and revenue plummet”. With capital scarce, every dollar must earn the most it can: 35 cents of gross profit under the Harley name, 5 cents at Buell. The crisis also makes the bear case the most likely: a loss of $23.5m.

    Freeze investment and invite offers. Sell if the proceeds exceed the value of keeping Buell, which is below zero in every scenario.The sale test. Sell rather than close, to keep the value of its patented engineering and its riders who would never buy a Harley.

    6 · Conclusion

    Harley does not have a brand problem but a relevance problem, and it can solve it with assets it already owns

    Harley’s customers are ageing and young adults are not replacing them. They already rate Harley a leadership brand, but only 16% feel it is for them: the dealership intimidates them, the chrome image is not theirs, and they believe a Harley costs three times its real price. The answer is to make Harley-Davidson itself young again, not to rely on Buell. We recommend seven moves, all within the CMO’s 27 months.

    #MoveWhat Harley does
    1A bike they recogniseGroup the dark models into one Harley-Davidson line, led by the Iron 883 at $7,499 from January 2009
    2A price they believeLead every advert and showroom with the real entry price: “from $6,699”
    3A dealership that welcomes themYoung-adult Garage Parties, test rides, younger advisers and trained staff, piloted in a few dealers first
    4A community they can joinRider’s Edge training with the first purchase and H.O.G.19 rides for new riders
    5A brand they see themselves inFilms that show young riders on dark Harleys, as Easy Rider and Terminator 2 did for the Boomers, and a young apparel collection with a free test ride
    6Money and data behind itRaise Outreach marketing from 17% towards 30% as pilots prove results, protect the Core’s events, and report sales by customer segment
    7Sell BuellThe 1125R destroyed $10.5m and keeping Buell is worth −$14.6m; in a crisis every dollar must go where it earns 35%, not 5%

    Moves 1–6 need under $1 million of new capital and are funded from the existing marketing budget, so they hold up in the crisis. Success will show in four numbers: young adult share above 22%, the “can relate” score from 16% to 24% or more, the median buyer age (47) no longer rising, and no loss of Core customers.

    Main uncertainties. Buell’s real price and costs are not in the case (the DCF tests a wider range); whether films and apparel turn into test rides must be proved in the pilots; and the depth of the crisis. The main risks, dealer resistance and Core backlash, are handled by central funding of pilots and by leaving the Core’s bikes and events untouched (Appendix E).

    Appendix

    Supporting analysis

    A. Prioritisation and timing of the options

    Each option was scored on the four prioritisation criteria from class: impact, ability to influence, effort and strategic fit. The options in blue score well on all four. The new youth platform fails on ability to influence, because it would arrive too late; Buell as the gateway fails on impact and fit, because it does not make the Harley brand younger; emerging markets fail on impact for this problem, because they do not renew the US customer base.

    Time decides the product question: a new platform would arrive 21 months after the average CMO has left.

    These changes should first be piloted in a small number of dealerships. With a recession starting and dealers sceptical of Outreach spending, a large untested campaign would be a risky response. A pilot creates the evidence dealers need: visits, test rides and conversion rates for under-35s, measured against the rest of the network.

    Rebalancing marketing effort. Only 17% of planned US marketing in 2007 targeted Outreach groups, while the case states that attracting young adults is the brand’s biggest open question. The share should rise in stages towards about 30%,20 funded within the existing budget and released only as pilots prove conversion. The Core’s rallies, H.O.G. support and dealer events are ring-fenced so that the shift does not read as abandonment.

    20 Author assumption; the case gives no target. The final figure should be set from pilot results.

    C. Size of the prize, films and apparel

    The case does not give the size of the young adult market, so the prize is shown per unit of success. Every 1,000 young adults who buy an Iron 883 bring about $7.5 million of revenue and about $2.6 million of gross profit at the brand-average margin.21 Buell would need about $52 million of revenue at its 5% margin to earn the same gross profit. The larger prize is lifetime value: a 25-year-old rider brought into the brand can trade up within it for decades, as the Boomers did.

    Films and apparel. The case says the Boomers bought Harleys “in fulfillment of dreams fed by films like Easy Rider and Terminator 2”. Films can do the same for the next generation, provided they show young riders on the dark bikes rather than older men on chrome choppers, the image young adults do not relate to. Clothing is already a sign of belonging in the Harley community; a young collection in the dark style is a low-cost first contact with the brand, much as Hard Rock Café T-shirts were worn by people with little interest in the restaurants. To make sure apparel leads to motorcycles, each young-collection purchase includes a free test ride or a Rider’s Edge discount. Both are funded from the shift of marketing towards Outreach and judged on test rides and the “can relate” score, not on merchandise sales.

    E. Risks and what-ifs
    What if…Response
    The recession deepensThe plan relies on existing bikes and an investment of less than $1 million; the lowest-priced models also suit tighter budgets. Pilots can pause without stranded costs.
    Dealers resist the changesFund the pilots centrally and show the conversion data before asking dealers to invest.
    Core customers feel abandonedKeep the chrome line, rallies and H.O.G. support unchanged, and present the dark line as a new family, not a new Harley.
    Young adults still choose sport bikesSome will. Harley does not need speed buyers; it needs the share who want authenticity and community, which its research shows exists.
    The Iron 883 is delayedThe existing dark models carry the line; the Iron 883 strengthens it but the plan does not depend on it.
    No buyer pays enough for BuellKeep Buell without new models and re-run the DCF against the 10% margin target. If the bear case applies, plan an orderly wind-down.

    What would change the recommendation

    Keeping and investing in Buell would become right if credible forecasts showed attractive future cash flows, real incremental customers who would never buy a Harley, or offers well below Buell’s value inside the group. Cultural marketing should be cut back if it generates apparel sales and publicity but no improvement in motorcycle consideration or dealer conversion.

It is a conservative assumption: Suzuki and Yamaha update their flagship sport bikes about every two years and replace them every four to five (GSX-R1000: new 2001, 2005, 2009, updated 2003, 2007, 2012; YZF-R1: new 1998, 2002, 2007, 2009, updated 2000, 2004, 2006, 2012; Bennetts BikeSocial, Wikipedia).
